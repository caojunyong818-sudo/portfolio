Overview

Time Boundary is a card-based serious game about the boundary between work and rest. In the alchemical world of Duskfire Workshop, players move through planning, execution, closure and rest while balancing limited resources, unfinished work and external commissions. The project makes decisions about when to start, how much to do and when to stop tangible and open to reflection.

Research: from a survey to a day in context

The exploratory research includes 38 questionnaire responses and seven interview records. The survey sample comprises 18 undergraduates, three postgraduates, three people with 1–3 years of work experience, 12 with more than five years, and two respondents in other categories. Interviews followed a recent day, asking how participants began tasks, responded to messages, stopped working and entered rest.

When asked whether unfinished tasks remained on their mind after work or class, 15 respondents answered “often” and five “almost always”: 20 of 38 in total. Another 15 answered “sometimes” and three “never”. These findings guided the interviews; this small exploratory sample does not establish population prevalence.

How interviews reframed the question

The initial survey analysis focused on difficulty ending work and guilt about resting. Interviews revealed a more varied picture: some people rested naturally, some needed a clear first step, and others resumed work when a new message activated a sense of responsibility. The design therefore focused on unresolved states: action had paused, but the next step, completion criteria or ownership remained unclear.

01

Before starting: a concrete first step

When a task is vague or choices are overwhelming, a reminder to focus does not provide an entry point. The design needs to make the next action visible.

02

After stopping: a state that can be resumed

Brief entertainment may divert attention while an unresolved task remains active. Closure should preserve progress, the reason for stopping, the next step and any waiting conditions.

03

When a message arrives: responsibility and consequences

Pressure depends on the responsibility a message activates; contact with friends can also be supportive. The key is to distinguish urgent requests from negotiable responsibilities and deferrable information.

04

Counterexamples: resting without difficulty

The interviews also included people who could rest comfortably. The game allows players to recognise their own patterns and explore different reasonable strategies.

Design opportunity: connect boundary decisions

An early review considered tools for recording, content generation, scheduling, focus timing, saving state and relaxation. Read alongside the interviews, it highlighted decisions that users still had to make between these functions: whether to accept an interruption, who owns the task, how much to do now, whether to stop, and how to resume later.

This led to a serious game in which choices can be tried and revisited. Limited resources and consequences across phases make trade-offs visible without requiring players to maintain another daily tracking system. The goal is to help players recognise boundaries around action and responsibility; transfer to everyday life remains to be evaluated.

Core loop: a purpose for every phase

The workshop gives abstract decisions a physical metaphor: preparing ingredients represents planning, brewing represents execution, sealing represents closure, and cooling represents rest. Each phase has its own deck, and choices in one phase affect the resources and space available in the next.

01

Plan · Find the next step

Organise information and reveal executable actions before committing resources to an unclear task.

02

Execute · Decide how much is enough

Balance the target against available resources and consider the marginal cost of another card.

03

Close · Leave a resumable state

Seal used execution cards to create room for the following phase.

04

Rest · Recover for what comes next

See how unresolved actions occupy rest space and reconsider the balance between progress and recovery.

Four research situations, four mechanics

01

Unclear starting point → Reveal actions

Execution cards begin face down. Planning cards reveal or organise actions, while committing blindly immediately consumes resources. Clarifying the next step becomes a meaningful choice.

02

Unbalanced investment → Additional cost

The target range is partly uncertain. After reaching its displayed lower bound, further execution costs more energy and occupies rest-card space. Players must decide when to move on to closure.

03

Unfinished closure → Seal cards

Used execution cards need to be sealed with closure cards. Unsealed cards enter the rest hand, occupy slots and weaken recovery, making unfinished work visible through its consequences.

04

External responsibility → Schedule commissions

Choose commissions at map nodes and prioritise them against preparation in research, execution, verification and delivery. Limited deferrals create trade-offs between priority and manageable responsibility.

Active choices and external support

In the rules, cards represent actions that require a decision in the moment, such as searching, breaking down a task, checking, recording and locking scope. Relics represent ongoing support such as templates, autosave, context anchors and notification filters. These supports reduce effort without deciding for the player whether the work is sufficient or whether to stop.

Task score and energy have different roles: score determines whether the necessary target has been met, while remaining energy affects future actions. Overinvestment, missing closure and interruptions to rest are presented as process events. This separates the outcome from the cost of reaching it.

Prototype and next steps

The prototype uses UE5 C++ and runtime UMG interfaces. It includes branching maps, encounters, shops, rest and boss nodes, with card and resource state carried across levels. The images below show the actual menu, encounter, map and commission interfaces.

Card parameters and combinations were first organised in spreadsheets, then refined through rule feedback, Chinese prompts and interface interactions in the prototype. Future playtests will examine whether players can identify a next step, explain a stopping decision, preserve a resumable state and prioritise external commissions. The current work demonstrates design and prototype iteration; it does not yet establish behavioural improvement.
